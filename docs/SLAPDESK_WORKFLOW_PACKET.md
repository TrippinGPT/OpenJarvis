# Trippin Labs SlapDesk Workflow Packet

## Purpose

This is the first practical SlapDesk workflow packet.

It is meant to help a producer, artist, or engineer move a session from rough idea to bounce with cleaner decisions and cleaner handoff.

It is workflow support only.

- no DAW automation
- no plugin installers
- no audio capture
- no autonomous producing

SlapDesk helps the room stay organized. It does not replace taste.

## Suggested first-use flow

Use this order for a normal session:

1. Cookup
2. Pocket
3. Low End
4. Slap Map
5. Trunk Check
6. Stem Run
7. Mix Sheet
8. Bounce Check

If the session is moving fast, use the minimum-viable pass near the end of this doc.

## 1. Cookup

### Purpose

Get the idea down fast and decide what the record is trying to be before the session gets crowded.

### What to listen/check for

- main bounce or emotional center
- strongest melodic or rhythmic hook
- whether the drums and core idea actually belong together
- whether the record has a clear mood or is just stacked sounds

### Common mistakes

- adding sounds before defining the record identity
- mistaking loudness for excitement
- building eight ideas at once
- saving no note about what the beat is actually supposed to do

### Quick checklist

- What is the record trying to be?
- What is the main hook?
- What is the main drum identity?
- What part should never be lost later?
- Can the session be described in one sentence?

### Good enough to move on

- the idea is recognizable
- the hook or core bounce exists
- the session has a clear direction
- there is at least one note explaining what the record is aiming for

## 2. Pocket

### Purpose

Check groove, movement, and arrangement feel so the record breathes instead of just playing.

### What to listen/check for

- swing or straight feel
- where the groove actually lives
- whether the arrangement creates movement or just repetition
- silence, space, and timing around the drums

### Common mistakes

- over-quantizing life out of the groove
- filling every space with sound
- building a loop that never develops
- treating arrangement like mute automation instead of feel design

### Quick checklist

- Does the groove move naturally?
- Is there enough space between major elements?
- Does the arrangement evolve?
- Does the bounce survive outside the eight-bar loop?
- What part feels stiff?

### Good enough to move on

- the record has movement
- the arrangement breathes
- the groove feels intentional
- the session is no longer stuck in loop syndrome

## 3. Low End

### Purpose

Make kick, bass, and sub decisions intentional before the mix stage has to clean up a mess.

### What to listen/check for

- kick and bass relationship
- sub consistency
- low-end mud
- where the bottom disappears or piles up

### Common mistakes

- kick and bass fighting for the same moment
- adding sub without defining the low-end owner
- chasing weight with loudness instead of control
- ignoring small speaker translation

### Quick checklist

- Who owns the bottom?
- Does the kick read clearly?
- Does the bass support or smother the record?
- Are there muddy overlaps?
- Does the low end still make sense at lower volume?

### Good enough to move on

- kick and bass roles are clear
- low end feels controlled, not just heavy
- there is no obvious low-end collision dominating the session

## 4. Slap Map

### Purpose

Create the session game plan before handoff or deeper mix prep.

### What to listen/check for

- hero elements
- emotional path
- what needs to stay forward
- what can stay support-only

### Common mistakes

- sending a session without hierarchy
- assuming the engineer will guess the important parts
- treating every element like a lead
- forgetting the difference between vibe and priority

### Quick checklist

- What are the hero elements?
- What is the emotional path of the record?
- Which elements are support-only?
- What must stay up front?
- What can be simplified or cut?

### Good enough to move on

- the session has clear priorities
- the record’s emotional path is understood
- handoff decisions are easier because the map exists

## 5. Trunk Check

### Purpose

Test whether the record actually hits outside the creative bubble.

### What to listen/check for

- energy translation
- whether the bounce still feels alive
- whether the low end and groove survive outside studio headphones
- whether the record feels exciting in a normal playback context

### Common mistakes

- only checking on one listening setup
- confusing “loud” with “hard”
- ignoring whether the record feels flat in real playback
- keeping dead sections because the producer is too used to them

### Quick checklist

- Does it still knock?
- Does it still move?
- Does the hook still read?
- Does the low end still make sense?
- What dies outside the main setup?

### Good enough to move on

- the record translates acceptably
- the energy is still there
- major translation failures are already obvious and noted

## 6. Stem Run

### Purpose

Export and organize the session so the next handoff is clean instead of painful.

### What to listen/check for

- stem completeness
- naming consistency
- whether grouped exports still make sense
- whether the engineer can open the package without guessing

### Common mistakes

- random file names
- missing stems
- inconsistent export settings
- sending clutter instead of a handoff

### Quick checklist

- Are all critical stems exported?
- Are names readable and consistent?
- Are versions clear?
- Are muted or throwaway parts excluded?
- Could another person understand this folder in one minute?

### Good enough to move on

- the session package is organized
- stems are named clearly
- the engineer or collaborator can work without translation labor

## 7. Mix Sheet

### Purpose

Give the engineer a clean brief that preserves intent and avoids guesswork.

### What to listen/check for

- what should stay featured
- what must stay emotional
- what needs fixing versus what needs protecting
- whether the notes are useful or just vague preferences

### Common mistakes

- “make it hard” with no specifics
- no note about lead priorities
- no note about what should stay dirty versus cleaned up
- forgetting to mention references, transition issues, or arrangement concerns

### Quick checklist

- What is the most important element?
- What should stay aggressive, soft, wide, or raw?
- What is the one thing the engineer should not ruin?
- What needs cleanup first?
- Are there references or target notes?

### Good enough to move on

- the engineer has clear intent
- the mix priorities are visible
- the note sheet reduces confusion instead of adding it

## 8. Bounce Check

### Purpose

Confirm the final output still feels like the record that was intended.

### What to listen/check for

- missing impact
- vocal or lead balance shifts
- low-end collapse
- brittle highs
- whether the final bounce still feels like the mapped target

### Common mistakes

- approving a bounce because the process is finally over
- forgetting to compare the bounce to the original session intention
- missing obvious distortion or balance collapse
- not checking the beginning, middle, and ending deliberately

### Quick checklist

- Does the bounce still feel like the intended record?
- Did the hook survive?
- Did the low end survive?
- Did anything harsh or muddy show up?
- Is this ready to send, post, or archive?

### Good enough to move on

- the bounce matches intent closely enough
- no obvious issue is left unnoted
- the record is ready for send-out or revision decision

## Mock session flow

This is one realistic example of how SlapDesk should be used.

### Rough idea

- Start in FL Studio with drums, bass, and one melodic hook.
- Write one sentence for the record:
  - “Dark, bouncy, late-night record with a simple lead and hard low end.”

### Arrangement and groove

- Loop the main idea.
- Check whether the groove actually moves or only feels good in the first eight bars.
- Trim any sound that weakens bounce.
- Add transition points only where they help movement.

### Low-end check

- Decide whether the kick or bass owns the bottom at the important moments.
- Reduce collisions before the mix stage.
- Check the record at lower volume for mud and balance.

### Structure and mix prep

- Mark hero elements.
- Note what needs to stay up front.
- Export stems with clear names.
- Fill out the mix sheet with priorities and cleanup notes.

### Export and bounce sanity

- Listen to the bounce as a record, not just as a file.
- Check hook, low end, and energy.
- Decide:
  - ready to send
  - ready for one revision pass
  - not ready yet

## Bounce-check checklist

Use this before approving the final bounce:

```text
SLAPDESK BOUNCE CHECK

[ ] Hook still lands
[ ] Low end still reads clearly
[ ] Kick and bass still feel controlled
[ ] No obvious harshness
[ ] No obvious mud
[ ] Main energy still translates
[ ] Lead element is still where it should be
[ ] Beginning feels clean
[ ] Middle still moves
[ ] Ending still lands
[ ] File/version naming is correct
[ ] Ready to send / revise / archive decision is made
```

## Minimum viable pass

If the session is moving fast, do this shorter version:

1. Cookup
   - define the record sentence
   - lock the main hook

2. Pocket
   - fix obvious stiffness
   - make the loop move

3. Low End
   - define bottom ownership
   - remove obvious collision

4. Slap Map
   - name hero elements
   - note what must stay up front

5. Stem Run + Mix Sheet
   - export cleanly
   - write the handoff

6. Bounce Check
   - confirm the final bounce still hits

## Boundaries

SlapDesk remains:

- AI-assisted workflow support
- not a replacement for taste
- not autonomous producing
- not full DAW control
- not plugin or platform automation

That boundary stays in place even when the templates become more detailed.
